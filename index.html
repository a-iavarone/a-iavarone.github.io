Job Market Paper

US–China Tech War

With Runpu Li

Abstract

We study the effect of export controls on the Chinese economy and their implications for US-China competition. We collect the rules through which the United States controls the export of high-tech goods to foreign countries, most notably China. From these rules we take the what and the when of the controls: which products are restricted, and when each restriction is introduced. Then, by means of a language model, for each controlled product we list the Chinese firms that make it, the makers, and the Chinese firms that buy it, the buyers. We study firm-level outcomes around the tightenings of these controls. A simple framework would lead us to expect the controls to be good for the Chinese makers, since they give them a form of protection, and bad for the Chinese buyers, since they lose access to a supplier of a critical input. In fact, in event studies from 2000 to 2024, the Chinese makers of a tightened product grow: three years on, their log sales are 0.226, their log R&D 0.196 and their log inventories 0.317 higher than those of comparable firms of the same size and growth. However, the Chinese buyers grow as well, with log sales 0.174 higher in the year of the tightening. We make sense of these results with a two-country quality-ladder model with two levels of production, intermediate (the makers of the controlled product) and final (its buyers): if the controls fall on product lines where China's final producer has just made a large innovation, the event studies detect gains for the Chinese buyers after a control arrives. Calibrated to China's catch-up over the dual-use product lines, from 42.0 years of US growth behind in 2000 to 9.1 in 2024, the model says that the controls slow the catch-up by 2.5 years by 2024, holding back China's final producers and advancing its intermediate producers.
